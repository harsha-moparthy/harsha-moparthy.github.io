Most "think better" content is repackaged common sense. The sources below are the ones that
survived contact with actual decisions — each changed something concrete about how I work.

## Start here

- **[Farnam Street](https://fs.blog/)** — Shane Parrish's blog is the best single starting point on
  clear thinking, full stop. Begin with [the core mental models
  post](https://fs.blog/mental-models/), then wander. The writing is dense with ideas and light on
  filler, and the archive rewards slow reading. The FS newsletter (Brain Food) is one of the few I
  keep after years of pruning.
- **[Clear Thinking](https://fs.blog/clear/)** by Shane Parrish — the book distills the blog's
  decade of material into one pass: recognizing when a moment *is* a decision, defaults that hijack
  you (emotion, ego, social pressure, inertia), and building safeguards instead of relying on
  willpower in the moment.

## The deep cuts

- **[Poor Charlie's Almanack](https://www.stripe.press/poor-charlies-almanack)** — Charlie Munger's
  collected talks. "The Psychology of Human Misjudgment" alone is worth the book: twenty-five
  cognitive tendencies, each with real consequences attached. Stripe Press's edition is free to
  read online.
- **[Superforecasting](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/227815/superforecasting-by-philip-e-tetlock-and-dan-gardner/)**
  by Philip Tetlock — what measurably good judgment looks like: small updates, explicit
  probabilities, keeping score. The single most transferable habit: write the forecast down
  *before* the outcome arrives.
- **[Thinking in Bets](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/552885/thinking-in-bets-by-annie-duke/)**
  by Annie Duke — separates decision quality from outcome quality. "Resulting" — judging a decision
  by how it happened to turn out — is the most common analysis error in engineering postmortems,
  and this book names it.

## Habits that make the reading stick

- **Keep a decision journal.** FS has [a template](https://fs.blog/decision-journal/). For any
  decision that matters: the situation, what you expect to happen and with what probability, how
  you feel. Review quarterly. It is humbling and it works — memory rewrites itself; the journal
  doesn't.
- **Invert, always invert.** Before optimizing for success, list what would guarantee failure and
  avoid it. Cheaper than brilliance and more reliable.
- **Write to find out what you think.** Paul Graham's
  [*Putting Ideas into Words*](http://paulgraham.com/words.html) makes the case in four pages: a
  half-formed idea feels complete until you try to write it down.

One warning: collecting frameworks is its own procrastination. Pick one habit — the journal is the
highest-leverage — and run it for a quarter before adding anything else.
